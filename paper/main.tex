\begin{abstract}
Choosing a runtime and numeric precision for an edge vision model is usually justified by latency alone. We present
an open, fully regenerable benchmark that measures, for every (model, runtime, precision) configuration, pooled latency
percentiles with bootstrap confidence intervals and a between-session stability verdict, a measured end-to-end stage
breakdown, the numerical parity of all outputs against a PyTorch FP32 reference, and task quality re-evaluated through
the runtime itself, against acceptance gates fixed before the run. Evaluating a compact anchor-free detector and a
convolutional autoencoder, both trained from scratch, in PyTorch (CPU, GPU) and ONNX Runtime (CPU) at FP32, FP16 and
static INT8, we find that INT8 is model-dependent ($\res{det-ort-cpu-int8-speedupfp}\times$ faster and
$\res{det-ort-cpu-int8-sizered}$\% smaller for the detector, but only $\res{ae-ort-cpu-int8-speedupfp}\times$ for the
autoencoder), that default TF32 GPU math breaks FP32 parity, and that host-side pre- and post-processing dominate the
detector's end-to-end latency ($\res{det-pt-gpu-fp32-e2e}$\,ms versus $\res{det-pt-gpu-fp32-p50}$\,ms model path).
We also test quantization-aware NMS calibration: re-tuning post-processing thresholds raises detection $F_1$ from
$\res{qa0-f1def}$ to $\res{qa0-f1cal}$ for every precision, but INT8-specific thresholds give no significant gain
over reusing the FP32-calibrated ones ($\Delta F_1 = \res{qa3-dtr}$, 95\% CI [$\res{qa3-dtrlo}$, $\res{qa3-dtrhi}$]),
while decision-flip attribution shows that INT8 changes $\res{fl3-cal-rate}$ of individual detection decisions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Deploying a vision model means choosing a runtime and a numeric precision, and the choice is usually justified by
latency numbers alone. Two things are easy to get wrong in that process. First, latency on a general-purpose host is
noisy: medians move between measurement sessions, and a single session can make an optimization look better or
worse than it is. Second, lower precision changes \emph{decisions}, not only tensors: for a detector, a small logit
drift can push boxes across the confidence threshold or past non-maximum suppression (NMS), which an aggregate metric
such as mAP may hide.

This paper presents an open benchmark that treats latency, numerical parity and task quality as one measurement.
For every (model, runtime, precision) configuration it reports pooled latency percentiles with bootstrap confidence
intervals and a between-session stability verdict, a measured end-to-end stage breakdown, the parity of every output
tensor against a PyTorch FP32 reference, and the task metric re-evaluated through that exact runtime, each checked
against acceptance thresholds fixed in configuration files before the run. On top of this we evaluate
\emph{Q-Aware NMS}---re-selecting the post-processing thresholds per precision on a labeled calibration split---and
\emph{decision-flip attribution}, which decomposes how reduced precision changes individual detections.

Contributions: (i) a session-based protocol and open implementation in which every reported number is regenerated
by one command; (ii) an evaluation of FP32, FP16 and static INT8 inference in PyTorch and ONNX Runtime~\cite{onnxruntime}
for a compact anchor-free detector and a convolutional autoencoder; (iii) a statistically tested assessment of
per-precision threshold calibration; and (iv) a detection-level flip analysis of quantization.

All models are trained from scratch on procedurally generated data, and all measurements come from a single laptop
workstation. The results therefore characterize the runtimes and the methodology, not real-world accuracy or embedded
hardware; Section~\ref{sec:threats} discusses these limits.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Quantization.} Integer quantization maps tensors to 8-bit values via a scale and zero point~\cite{jacob2018quantization}.
Post-training quantization (PTQ) calibrates the ranges on a small dataset without retraining and is the common
deployment path~\cite{krishnamoorthi2018quantizing,nagel2021white,gholami2022survey}; surveys of compression and
acceleration discuss the resulting accuracy--efficiency trade-offs~\cite{deng2020model}.
\textbf{Dense detection.} One-stage detectors predict boxes on a dense grid~\cite{redmon2018yolov3,tian2019fcos} and
rely on NMS~\cite{neubeck2006efficient,bodla2017soft}; focal and quality-aware classification losses~\cite{lin2017focal,li2020gfl}
and IoU-based regression losses~\cite{rezatofighi2019giou} are standard. Evaluation follows the COCO protocol~\cite{lin2014coco}.
\textbf{Anomaly detection.} Reconstruction-based models score defects by reconstruction error and are evaluated with
image AUROC and the per-region-overlap (PRO) curve~\cite{bergmann2019mvtec}.
\textbf{Measurement.} Robust statistics such as the median absolute deviation~\cite{leys2013detecting}, bootstrap
confidence intervals~\cite{efron1994introduction} and rank tests with family-wise error control~\cite{wilcoxon1945individual,holm1979simple}
are standard tools that inference benchmarks rarely report.

\section{Benchmark Design}

\subsection{Workloads and data}
The \textbf{detector} is a compact anchor-free network (\res{det-params}\,M parameters): a five-stage strided
Conv-BN-SiLU backbone and three heads at strides 8/16/32 that predict per-location (left, top, right, bottom)
distances~\cite{tian2019fcos}, decoded inside the exported graph to an output of shape $[1, 4{+}2, 8400]$ for a
$640{\times}640$ input. It is trained for \res{det-trainsteps} steps with a quality focal loss~\cite{li2020gfl} and a
GIoU loss~\cite{rezatofighi2019giou}. The \textbf{autoencoder} (\res{ae-params}\,M parameters) has a four-stage
strided-convolution encoder, a $1{\times}1$ bottleneck and a transposed-convolution decoder; it is trained for
\res{ae-trainsteps} steps on defect-free textures only, and its anomaly map is the per-pixel mean absolute
reconstruction error.

Data are procedural: two-class shapes (rectangles and ellipses) with unlabeled distractors on noisy textured
backgrounds of four resolutions, and sinusoidal surface textures with scratch, stain and texture-dropout defects with
pixel masks. Train, validation, calibration and test splits come from disjoint seed ranges, and calibration/test file
overlap is verified to be zero. The validation split is used only to select the anomaly-score smoothing
($\sigma{=}\res{ae-sigma}$\,px Gaussian, mean of the top \res{ae-topk}\% pixels). The test split contains
\res{qa-test-images} detection images (\res{qa-test-boxes} boxes) and 100 normal plus 100 anomalous textures.

\subsection{Artifacts and runtimes}
Models are exported to ONNX (opset 17) and simplified; FP16 graphs keep FP32 inputs and outputs; INT8 graphs use ONNX
Runtime static QDQ quantization with MinMax calibration on the calibration split, per-channel symmetric weights, and
Conv, ConvTranspose, MatMul and Gemm quantized. The matrix covers PyTorch eager on CPU and CUDA (FP32, FP16) and ONNX
Runtime on CPU (FP32, FP16, INT8). ONNX Runtime CUDA and TensorRT back ends are implemented and are run automatically
when available; neither was available on the benchmark host.

\subsection{Measurement protocol}
\textbf{Model-path latency} is measured on device-resident inputs: per session, \res{proto-warmupiterations} untimed
and \res{proto-timediterations} timed passes with garbage collection disabled, timed with CUDA events on GPU (the
call does not synchronize the host) and \texttt{perf\_counter\_ns} on CPU. Each configuration runs
\res{proto-stabilitysessions} sessions separated by a cache-eviction write and a cooldown; if the coefficient of
variation (CV) of the session medians exceeds 0.04, two supplemental sessions are added once. Percentiles are computed
over the pooled iterations with percentile-bootstrap 95\% CIs ($B{=}2000$), and a configuration is \emph{stable} if
the CV of all session medians is at most 0.05.
\textbf{End-to-end latency} times decoding, preprocessing, inference through the host-array API (including
host--device copies) and post-processing separately over \res{proto-e2etimediterations} iterations.
\textbf{Parity} compares all output tensors with PyTorch FP32 on CPU for a real test image (maximum relative error
and cosine similarity), and \textbf{quality} re-evaluates the full test split through the runtime under test.
Acceptance thresholds per precision tier are fixed in configuration files (relative error $10^{-4}/5{\cdot}10^{-3}/0.1$;
quality drop $0.001/0.005/0.02$ for FP32/FP16/INT8).

\subsection{Q-Aware NMS and decision flips}
For each detector configuration, raw outputs on the calibration split (\res{qa-calib-images} images) are cached and
$(\tau_c^*, \tau_i^*) = \arg\max F_1$ is searched over $\tau_c \in \{0.10,\dots,0.95\}$ and
$\tau_i \in \{0.05,\dots,0.70\}$ in steps of 0.05 (252 points); ties keep the default $(0.25, 0.45)$. On the test split we
report micro-$F_1$ at both operating points, a paired image-level bootstrap 95\% CI of $\Delta F_1$, and a two-sided
Wilcoxon signed-rank test on per-image error counts (false positives + false negatives), Holm--Bonferroni corrected
across configurations.

Decision flips compare a configuration's detections with those of the PyTorch FP32 reference. Boxes are matched
greedily in score order (IoU $\ge 0.5$, same class); unmatched reference boxes are \emph{lost true positives} or
\emph{suppressed false positives} and unmatched target boxes are \emph{new true positives} or \emph{new false positives},
judged against ground truth. With $M$ matched boxes and $F$ flips, the flip rate is $\Phi = F / (M + F)$.

\subsection{Models and parity}
The trained detector reaches mAP@50 \res{det-map50} (mAP@50--95 \res{det-map5095}) on the test split and the autoencoder
an image AUROC of \res{ae-imageauroc} (AU-PRO \res{ae-aupro}). ONNX export is exact up to floating-point reordering
(maximum relative error $\le 10^{-6}$) and FP16 stays within $5\cdot 10^{-3}$ for both models. PyTorch's default TF32
convolutions on the GPU produced a relative error of $1.05\cdot 10^{-4}$, failing the FP32 gate; the FP32 rows therefore
use strict IEEE FP32 (TF32 disabled), which costs speed: the autoencoder takes \res{ae-pt-gpu-fp32-p50}\,ms in FP32 versus
\res{ae-pt-gpu-fp16-p50}\,ms in FP16.

\subsection{Static INT8}
The INT8 calibration method was chosen on the calibration split among MinMax, Percentile and Entropy; Percentile won for
both models. With symmetric activation quantization, ONNX Runtime's Entropy ranges reduce to the MinMax scales (the
resulting graphs are byte-identical), because the scale is set by the larger of the two asymmetric thresholds. For the
detector, INT8 is $\res{det-ort-cpu-int8-speedupfp}\times$ faster than ORT FP32 and the file is
\res{det-ort-cpu-int8-sizered}\% smaller (\res{det-int8-mb} vs \res{det-fp32-mb}\,MB), but its outputs deviate strongly
(maximum relative error \res{det-ort-cpu-int8-relerr}), failing the parity gate. Its test mAP@50 nevertheless rose to
\res{det-ort-cpu-int8-q} (\res{det-ort-cpu-int8-qdelta}): clipping changed the ranking of the many low-confidence boxes.
We report this as a deviation, not an improvement; it would not necessarily transfer to other data. For the autoencoder
INT8 is \res{ae-ort-cpu-int8-sizered}\% smaller but only $\res{ae-ort-cpu-int8-speedupfp}\times$ faster, and changes
image AUROC by \res{ae-ort-cpu-int8-qdelta}, within the 0.02 allowance.

\section{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:threats}
\textbf{Synthetic data and small models.} Accuracy numbers describe a controlled synthetic task; they show whether a
runtime or precision preserves the trained model's behavior, not how a production detector performs.
\textbf{Host noise.} Measurements were taken on a laptop under WSL2 with other system activity; the stability verdicts
and between-session spread are part of the result rather than something removed by outlier filtering. The bootstrap
CIs resample iterations and therefore understate between-session drift.
\textbf{Hardware scope.} A laptop CPU and GPU are not embedded accelerators; INT8 speedups in particular depend on the
CPU's integer instructions and on ONNX Runtime's kernel coverage.
\textbf{Back-end coverage.} ONNX Runtime CUDA and TensorRT were not available on the host, so GPU comparisons are
limited to PyTorch.

\section{Conclusion}
Treating latency, parity and task quality as one gated measurement changes several conclusions one would draw from
latency alone: the faster CPU runtime depends on the model, static INT8 can be fast yet numerically far from FP32 while
its aggregate metric moves in either direction, FP32 on a modern GPU is TF32 unless disabled, and pre- and
post-processing dominate end-to-end latency. Calibrating post-processing thresholds matters far more than making them
precision-specific: on this workload the FP32-calibrated thresholds transfer to FP16 and INT8 without measurable loss,
although INT8 still alters a substantial share of individual detections.

\url{https://github.com/sengarutk/onnx-quant-benchmark}; \texttt{make pipeline} regenerates every number in this paper.
